A child who chants “one two three four five” at speed has learned a song. A child who counts five biscuits and stops at five has learned mathematics. These are different achievements, and mixing them up is the most common reason counting practice stalls.

Counting to 100 is really four skills stacked on top of each other. Here is each one, and what actually moves it.

## Skill 1: Rote counting (the song)

This is saying the number words in order. It comes first, it comes easily, and it is worth much less than it looks.

**What helps:** repetition set to rhythm. Count stairs on the way up. Count while brushing teeth. Count the seconds in a hug. The point is frequency, not concentration.

**Where it breaks:** almost always at **13 to 19**. More on that below.

## Skill 2: One-to-one correspondence

This is the real thing: one number word per object, no skipping, no doubling up. A child with this skill touches each grape as they count it and knows the last number said is the answer.

**What helps:** making them *touch*. Pointing is not enough at three — the finger drifts ahead of the voice. Move each object into a new pile as it is counted.

Good props: buttons, pasta, socks out of the laundry, steps, stairs, biscuits. Real objects beat pictures, because a picture can be miscounted without consequence and a biscuit cannot.

<Callout title="The test that tells you where you are">
Put out seven objects. Ask your child to count them. Then ask, without recounting, “so how many are there?” A child who has to count again has the song but not the concept. That is fine — it just tells you which skill to practise.
</Callout>

## Skill 3: The teens (where nearly every child gets stuck)

English numbers between 11 and 19 are genuinely badly designed. “Fourteen” says the four before the ten. “Eleven” and “twelve” say nothing useful at all. Children who breeze through 1–10 routinely stall for weeks here, and it is not their fault.

**What helps:**

- Treat the teens as their own unit. Practise 11–20 on its own, away from 1–10.
- Say the structure out loud: *“Fourteen. That's four and ten.”*
- Use ten objects in a line plus loose extras, so “thirteen” is visibly ten-and-three.

Hindi and Marathi speakers hit a different version of the same wall: the numbers from ११ to १८ each have their own irregular form, so there is even less pattern to lean on. The fix is the same — treat them as a block to be learned, not derived.

## Skill 4: The decade jumps

From 20 onward, English becomes regular and generous: twenty-one through twenty-nine follow one obvious rule. Children usually pick up a decade in a day or two.

The friction is at the **turn** — going from 29 to 30, 39 to 40. The child knows the pattern inside a decade but not what comes next.

**What helps:** practise only the turns. Say “twenty-eight, twenty-nine…” and let them supply “thirty”. Do ten of those in a row. It is a two-minute drill and it clears the problem faster than counting to 100 repeatedly does.

## Activities worth your time

**Count the stairs, every time.** Free, daily, and it builds one-to-one correspondence because each step is a physical event.

**The laundry pile.** Sorting socks is counting with a purpose attached. Purpose is what makes a three-year-old keep going.

**Hundred-chart hopscotch.** Chalk a 10×10 grid. Call a number, they jump to it. This builds the *spatial* sense of number — that 60 is far from 6 — which pure counting never does.

**Count in two languages on alternate days.** Bilingual counting is not twice the work; it is the same concept with two labels, and it makes the structure of numbers more visible, not less. A child who counts to twenty in English and हिंदी notices that both languages do something strange in the teens and something sensible afterwards.

**Snack maths.** “Take three grapes.” “Now take two more. How many?” Addition arrives naturally out of counting when the objects are edible.

## What progress actually looks like

Roughly, and with enormous individual variation:

| Age | Typical counting |
| --- | --- |
| 2–3 | Rote counting to 10; one-to-one correspondence for 2–4 objects |
| 3–4 | Rote to 20; correspondence to about 10; recognises some numerals |
| 4–5 | Counting to 30–50; correspondence to 20; understands “how many” |
| 5–6 | Counting to 100; counting on from a number; simple addition |

If your child sits below this, it means nothing on its own. If they sit *above* it but cannot answer “how many”, they have the song without the maths — go back to skill 2.

## Frequently asked questions

### At what age can a child count to 100?

Most children can count to 100 between ages 5 and 6, though rote counting to 20 usually appears around ages 3 to 4. Counting accurately with objects — one number per item — develops more slowly than the ability to recite numbers in order.

### Why does my child get stuck on the teen numbers?

English teen numbers are irregular: "eleven" and "twelve" follow no pattern, and "fourteen" says the smaller number first. Practise 11–20 as its own block, and say the structure out loud — "fourteen is four and ten" — rather than relying on the pattern.

### What is one-to-one correspondence?

One-to-one correspondence is matching exactly one number word to each object counted, and understanding that the last number said is the total. It is the difference between reciting numbers and actually counting, and it usually develops between ages 3 and 5.

### Can my child learn to count in two languages at once?
