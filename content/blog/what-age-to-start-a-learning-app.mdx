Parents usually ask this the week a child becomes interested in the phone. The answers online are unhelpfully split between "never" and "the earlier the better", and neither is right.

Here is a straight answer by age, from people who make one of these apps — including where ours is the wrong choice.

## The short answer

Under two, no. Around two to three, a few minutes at a time, sitting with an adult, on one specific thing. From three to five it becomes genuinely useful for letters, numbers and vocabulary. From five it can support reading and early maths properly. The child's readiness matters more than the birthday.

## Under two: no

There is not much debate here. Both the WHO and the American Academy of Pediatrics advise against screens for under-eighteen-month-olds apart from video calls, and only minimal, shared use between eighteen and twenty-four months.

More to the point, there is nothing an app offers a one-year-old that a person cannot offer better. At that age language comes almost entirely from talking, being talked to, and being read to. An app cannot respond to a babble or follow where a child's eyes went.

If your eighteen-month-old is fascinated by the phone, video calls with family are the exception worth making — those are genuinely interactive, and they are also how a grandparent gets a relationship.

## Two to three: short, shared, specific

This is where an app can start to add something, with three conditions attached.

- **Short.** Five minutes, perhaps twice a day. Not a session, a moment.
- **Shared.** You sitting next to them, naming things out loud, is where most of the value is at this age.
- **Specific.** One topic — colours, or animals, or counting to five. Not a tour of everything.

What suits this age is tap-and-hear: the child touches something, it says a word, they hear it said correctly. That loop is real learning. What does not suit it is anything requiring a sequence of steps or reading an instruction.

## Three to five: the useful years

This is where a learning app earns its place. Children can now manage a short session alone, follow a simple game, and actually retain what they met yesterday.

Reasonable expectations: ten to fifteen minutes, most days, on letters, numbers, colours, shapes and words. This is also the age at which the [Hindi or Marathi Varnamala](/blog/learn-hindi-for-kids-varnamala-guide) is worth starting if that is a language at home — [two languages will not confuse them](/blog/will-two-languages-confuse-my-child).

## Five to eight: more, and more independent

Sessions can be longer and the content harder — blending sounds into words, numbers past twenty, sentences, early maths. Children this age can also choose for themselves what to practise, which is worth letting them do.

The thing worth protecting here is that it stays something they want to do, rather than homework with a different logo.

<Callout title="The number matters less than three other things">
Can your child <strong>tap and wait for a response</strong>, rather than swiping at random? Can they <strong>stop</strong> when a session ends without it ruining the hour? Is there an adult nearby <strong>some</strong> of the time? If all three are yes, they are ready. If any is no, wait a few months — it costs nothing.
</Callout>

## What an app is good at

Being honest about this is more useful than a sales pitch:

- **Saying things correctly, every time.** Letter sounds, number names, Varnamala letters — particularly if you are not confident pronouncing them yourself.
- **Patience.** It will do the same letter forty times without a sigh.
- **Short, finishable practice.** Something that ends is easier for a small child than something open-ended.
- **Being available** when you are cooking, driving, or out of patience.

## What an app cannot do

- **Conversation.** Vocabulary and grammar come from back-and-forth with people. No app substitutes for that.
- **Handwriting.** That is hands, pencils and paper, not a screen.
- **Playing with other children**, which is where a lot of early learning actually happens.
- **Noticing your child.** It cannot see that they are tired, or bored, or nearly getting it.

A child who uses a good app for ten minutes and is talked to all day will do well. The reverse will not.

## Signs you started too early

Stop and wait a few months if you see:

- Random swiping and tapping with no attention to what happens
- Frustration within a minute, most times
- A meltdown every time it ends, however the ending is handled
- No interest in it at all — which is simply information, not a problem
- It replacing play rather than fitting around it

None of these mean anything is wrong. They mean *not yet*, and a few months at this age is a long time.

## How to start well

- Sit with them for the first week
- Pick **one** topic and stay with it
- Agree the ending before you start — "two levels, then we stop"
- Keep it short enough that they want more
- Talk about it afterwards: *"which one was your favourite?"*
