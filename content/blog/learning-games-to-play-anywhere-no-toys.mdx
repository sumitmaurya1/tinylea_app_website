The train is two hours late. Or the clinic is running behind, or the power has gone and so has the fan, or you are at a wedding where the ceremony is taking longer than anyone promised.

Your child is bored, and you have nothing with you. No toys, no colouring, a phone at eleven percent.

You need less than you think. Every game below needs only your voice, teaches something real, and works in a queue, a car, a dark room or a crowd. Save this for the next time you are stuck.

## Letter games

### 1. I spy — with sounds, not names

*"I spy something beginning with /b/."* Say the **sound**, not the letter name — "b", not "bee" — because sounds are what reading actually uses. Bag, bottle, bench, baby. Take turns being the spy.

**Teaches:** letter sounds, listening

### 2. The name game

Pick the first sound of your child's name and find everything around you that starts with it. A child called Meera hunts for mango, mobile, mat and mirror. Children care about their own name more than any other word, and it shows.

**Teaches:** first sounds, vocabulary

### 3. Alphabet journey

On a road or train trip, find the letters A to Z in order on signs, shop boards, number plates and posters. First to spot **A** wins that round; then everyone hunts for **B**. The whole family can play, and grandparents are often surprisingly competitive.

**Teaches:** letter recognition

## Number games

### 4. Count the…

Pick one thing and count every one you pass: red cars, auto-rickshaws, cows, blue doors. Stop at ten and pick something new. Short counts done often beat one long count to a hundred.

**Teaches:** counting, one-to-one matching

### 5. How many fingers?

Hold up some fingers behind your back, then show them for one second. How many? Start at one to five. It trains a child to *see* a small number without counting — which is a skill school builds on.

**Teaches:** recognising small quantities

### 6. Number plate hunt

Find a **1**, then a **2**, then a **3**, on number plates or bus numbers. Older children can look for two numbers that add up to five.

**Teaches:** numeral recognition, early addition

<Callout title="The one rule that keeps it fun">
Stop while they still want to play. A game that ends on a high gets asked for again tomorrow; a game that ends in a sulk gets refused. Five minutes of eager playing is worth more than twenty of dragging.
</Callout>

## Colours and shapes

### 7. Colour hunt

*"Find three yellow things."* Everyone looks, everyone points. Then switch colours. For toddlers, stick to one colour for the whole game — it teaches the colour better than switching.

**Teaches:** colour names

### 8. Shape spotting

Windows are rectangles, clocks are circles, road signs are triangles. Ask *"how many corners?"* rather than just *"what shape?"* — counting corners is how children learn to recognise a shape however it is turned.

**Teaches:** shapes, counting sides and corners

## Language games

### 9. Two-language swap

Say a word in English; your child says it in Hindi or Marathi — then swap. *Water — पानी. Dog — कुत्ता* (Marathi: *कुत्रा*). *One, two, three — एक, दो, तीन* (Marathi: *एक, दोन, तीन*). It is the easiest way to keep a home language in daily use, and grandparents love being the referee.

**Teaches:** vocabulary in two languages

### 10. Opposites

*"I say big, you say…"* Small. Hot, cold. Up, down. Happy, sad. Then let your child give you the first word and see if you get it right. Deliberately getting one wrong gets a big laugh.

**Teaches:** vocabulary, opposites

## Memory and listening

### 11. I went to the market and bought…

*"I went to the market and bought a mango."* Your child repeats it and adds one: *"…a mango and a banana."* Keep adding until someone forgets. For younger children, three or four items is plenty.

**Teaches:** memory, sequencing, listening

### 12. Two-step Simon says

*"Simon says touch your nose and then clap."* Two actions, in order. It sounds simple and it is exactly the skill teachers most want children to have on day one of school: holding two instructions in mind at once.

**Teaches:** listening, following instructions

## Making them stick

- **No quizzing tone.** It is a game, not a test. If it starts to feel like homework, it has stopped working.
- **Let them win sometimes** — and lose sometimes. Both matter.
- **Let them be the leader.** A child who asks the question learns more than one who only answers.
- **Repeat favourites.** Children like the same game ten times. That repetition is the learning.

## When the games run out

Some waits are longer than any game. A three-hour flight, an overnight train, a hospital evening — sometimes a screen is the reasonable choice, and there is no need to feel guilty about it.

If you do reach for one, it is worth it being something your child *does* rather than something that plays at them, and something that still works when the signal is gone, because the moments you need it most are usually the ones with no Wi-Fi.
