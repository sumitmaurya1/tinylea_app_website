Most Indian families are raising children in at least two languages, often three: English at school, one language at home, sometimes another from grandparents.

Parents are rarely told how these languages actually differ to learn, which is a shame — because the differences decide what to teach first and what to leave until later.

## The short answer

English is the hardest of these to read and the easiest to find materials for. Hindi, Marathi and Gujarati are all **phonetic**: once a child knows the letters, they can read almost anything, because letters say what they say. Teach speaking before script in every language, and get one script comfortable before starting the second.

## The scripts are not the same

**Hindi and Marathi** share one script, **Devanagari** — the one with the horizontal line running along the top of the words. A child learning one gets a large head start on the other; the letters are nearly all shared, and most of the work transfers.

**Gujarati** has its own script, closely related to Devanagari but visually distinct — most noticeably, it does not have that connecting line along the top. A Gujarati-speaking child learning Devanagari later will find it familiar rather than foreign, but it is a second script, not a variation.

**English** uses the Latin alphabet, with the smallest character set of the four and by far the least predictable spelling.

## Why Indian scripts are easier to read

This surprises parents who assume English is simpler because there are only twenty-six letters.

In Hindi, Marathi and Gujarati, a letter reliably makes its sound. Once a child knows the letters and the vowel marks, they can read an unfamiliar word correctly on sight — the spelling tells them how to say it.

English does not do this. *Though, through, tough, cough* share four letters and four different sounds. An English reader has to learn patterns, exceptions and a pile of words that simply have to be memorised — which is why [phonics](/blog/how-to-teach-phonics-at-home) takes so long and needs teaching so carefully.

So: the Indian script is often the **quicker win** once a child is ready for letters at all. Many parents assume the opposite.

## Each language, briefly

### English

The alphabet is the easy part; the sounds are the work. Teach letter **sounds** alongside names, start with the letters in your child's name, and expect reading to take a year or more after the letters are known. Vocabulary matters as much as decoding — a child who reads a word they do not know has not really read it.

### Hindi

The Varnamala is organised by where each sound is made in the mouth, which makes it far easier to learn in rows than as a list. Teach it in blocks, by sound, not by reciting it start to finish — the [Hindi Varnamala guide](/blog/learn-hindi-for-kids-varnamala-guide) sets out the order.

### Marathi

Same script as Hindi, with some additional sounds and its own vocabulary. The [Barakhadi](/blog/learn-marathi-for-kids-barakhadi-varnamala) — consonants combined with each vowel — is where Marathi reading really begins, and it is worth starting only once the basic letters are comfortable.

### Gujarati

Its own script, with vowels (**સ્વર**) and consonants (**વ્યંજન**), and a **બારાખડી** that works on the same principle as the Marathi one: each consonant paired with each vowel in turn.

Counting starts એક, બે, ત્રણ, ચાર, પાંચ. As in Hindi and Marathi, the numbers past ten are largely irregular and need learning in small groups rather than worked out from a rule.

<Callout title="Speaking first, script second — in every language">
This is the single most common mistake. A child who is pushed into letters before they speak comfortably usually ends up disliking the language. Conversation, songs and stories come first; the script is a separate project that can start around five or six.
</Callout>

## Running two or three languages

- **Pick a pattern and keep it.** One parent one language, or one language at home and another outside. The pattern matters more than which one you choose.
- **Count the total.** A child with words spread across three languages is not behind — [two or three languages do not confuse children](/blog/will-two-languages-confuse-my-child).
- **Do not start two scripts in the same month.** Get one comfortable first. Which one goes first matters far less than not starting both at once.
- **Give each language a real job.** A language used only for scolding loses to the language used for stories and jokes.
- **Use the strongest speaker.** Grandparents are usually the best resource in the family, and [a weekly call in that language](/blog/how-grandparents-can-help-a-child-learn) does more than any lesson.

## A plan by age

- **2–3** — speaking only, in every language the family uses. Songs, names of things, lots of talk.
- **3–5** — English letters and sounds; the home-language alphabet by sound; counting in both.
- **5–6** — the Barakhadi in Marathi or Gujarati; blending in English; reading simple words.
- **6–8** — reading in both scripts, vocabulary, writing.

## Which script first?

If school will be English-medium, English letters usually need to come first simply because school demands them.

If the home language is at risk — especially for families outside India — the home script is worth starting first or alongside, because English will get plenty of support from the environment and the home language will not.

There is no universal right answer. The wrong answer is both at once, in the same month, from scratch.

## A note on materials

For English there is endless material. For Hindi and Marathi there is a reasonable amount. For Gujarati and other Indian languages there is considerably less, and families often end up making their own — which is worth knowing before you go looking.
