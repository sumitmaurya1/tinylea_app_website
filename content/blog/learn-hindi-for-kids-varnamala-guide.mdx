If your child is learning English letters at school and Hindi nowhere, you are in the majority — and the gap widens fast. A five-year-old who reads "CAT" fluently but cannot recognise **क** is not behind in Hindi; they simply have not started.

Starting is easier than most parents expect, because Devanagari is, in one important way, a friendlier script than the Latin alphabet.

## Why Devanagari is easier than English to read

English spelling is close to arbitrary. *Though, through, tough, cough* share four letters and four different sounds. Children spend years absorbing exceptions.

Devanagari does not work that way. It is close to **phonetic**: a letter reliably makes its sound, and a matra reliably modifies it. Once your child knows the letters and the matras, they can decode words they have never seen.

The trade-off is that there are more symbols to learn up front. That is a front-loaded cost, not a permanent one.

## What the Varnamala actually contains

The Hindi Varnamala has two parts, and they are taught in this order for a reason.

**Swar (स्वर) — the vowels.** अ आ इ ई उ ऊ ऋ ए ऐ ओ औ अं अः — thirteen of them. They come first because every consonant sound needs one.

**Vyanjan (व्यंजन) — the consonants.** क ख ग घ ङ, च छ ज झ ञ, and so on — thirty-three, grouped in rows of five.

Those rows are not arbitrary. Each row shares a place of articulation in the mouth: the **क** row is made at the back of the throat, the **प** row at the lips. Within a row the sounds move in a predictable pattern. You do not need to explain this to a four-year-old, but knowing it yourself explains why the traditional order is worth keeping.

<Callout title="Teach the rows, not the list">
The five-letter rows are a gift. A child who learns क ख ग घ ङ as a chunk has learned a group of related sounds, not five unrelated shapes. Do one row at a time and let it settle.
</Callout>

## A starting order that works

1. **Start with the swar** — अ आ इ ई उ ऊ. Six vowels, several weeks. These are the foundation for everything after.
2. **Then the first consonant row** — क ख ग घ. (ङ is rare in isolation; do not make it a hurdle.)
3. **Then rows your child has words for** — the **प** row gives पापा, the **म** row gives माँ. Emotional connection beats alphabetical order at this age.
4. **Then the rest of the rows**, one at a time.
5. **Then matras**, once the letters are solid.

Do not begin matras early. A child who is still unsure whether that shape is **घ** or **ध** cannot also absorb what happens when you hang a ी on the end.

## Where children stumble, and what to do

**Look-alike letters.** भ/म, घ/ध, ब/व, ड/ङ. The same rule as English b/d applies: never teach two look-alikes in the same week, and when a mix-up happens, name the *difference* rather than the error. *"That one's ध — it's open at the top. भ has the line across."*

**Matras arriving too early.** The matra system is genuinely elegant, but it is a second layer. Letters first.

**Pronunciation drift, especially in non-Hindi-speaking homes.** This is the big one. The aspirated/unaspirated distinction — क vs ख, त vs थ — is real and load-bearing, and an English-speaking parent may not hear it reliably, let alone produce it.

That last problem is exactly what audio solves. A child who can tap a letter and hear it pronounced correctly, on demand, dozens of times, gets a model that does not depend on the adult in the room.

## If Hindi isn't spoken at home

It still works. What changes is that you have to supply the exposure the household is not providing.

- **Ten minutes daily beats an hour on Sunday.** Language learning is a frequency game.
- **Attach letters to objects in the house.** Label the door दरवाज़ा, the window खिड़की. The child sees the word every day without a lesson happening.
- **Use audio for everything.** Do not model a sound you are not confident about; let the app or a fluent relative do it.
- **Get a grandparent involved.** A weekly video call where नानी names five objects is worth more than any worksheet.
- **Rhymes and songs.** Hindi nursery rhymes carry vocabulary and rhythm without feeling like study.

## How long it takes

For a child doing ten minutes most days:

| Stage | Rough timeline |
| --- | --- |
| All 13 swar recognised | 4–8 weeks |
| First 10–15 vyanjan | 2–3 months |
| Full Varnamala recognised | 6–12 months |
| Matras and simple words | 12–18 months |

Faster if Hindi is spoken at home. Slower if practice is weekly. Neither says anything about your child.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the Hindi Varnamala?

The Hindi Varnamala is the Hindi alphabet, written in the Devanagari script. It has 13 vowels (swar) such as अ, आ and इ, and 33 consonants (vyanjan) such as क, ख and ग, traditionally grouped into rows of five by where each sound is made in the mouth.

### At what age should a child start learning Hindi letters?

Children can begin recognising Hindi letters from around age 3, starting with the vowels. Ages 4 to 6 are ideal for working through the consonants, since children are already learning that written symbols carry sound.

### Should I teach swar or vyanjan first?

Teach the swar (vowels) first. Every consonant sound in Hindi combines with a vowel, so the vowels are the foundation. Move to the consonant rows once your child recognises अ आ इ ई उ ऊ reliably.

### How can my child learn Hindi if we don't speak it at home?
