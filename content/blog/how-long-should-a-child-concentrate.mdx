A common worry, usually arriving around four: *she can't sit still for anything. How will she cope at school?*

Then the same child spends forty minutes building something out of blocks without looking up.

Both of those are true at once, and understanding why is most of what a parent needs here.

## The short answer

Young children can concentrate for far longer on something they chose than on something you chose. For an adult-led activity, a few minutes at three and perhaps ten to fifteen by six is realistic. For something absorbing and self-chosen, much longer. A child who seems unable to focus is usually a child being asked to focus on the wrong thing, in the wrong conditions.

## The rule of thumb, and what it's worth

You will often see a figure quoted: roughly two to five minutes of focused attention per year of age. So six to fifteen minutes at three; ten to twenty-five at five.

Treat it as a rough guide, not a measurement. It gets repeated widely without much evidence behind the exact numbers, and real children vary enormously by day, task and mood. Its one genuine use is as a reality check: if you are expecting a four-year-old to sit through twenty minutes of letter practice, the expectation is the problem, not the child.

## Chosen attention and assigned attention

This is the distinction that explains almost everything.

**Chosen attention** — the blocks, the puddle, the thing they are currently obsessed with — can run very long, because the child is driving. They decide what happens next, the difficulty is self-adjusting, and stopping is their choice.

**Assigned attention** — what you want them to do — is much shorter, because none of that is true. Someone else set the task, the difficulty is fixed, and the end point belongs to an adult.

A child who does forty minutes of the first and four minutes of the second does not have an attention problem. They have a perfectly normal four-year-old's attention, pointed somewhere they did not pick.

<Callout title="Watch what they do, not what you asked">
Before worrying about attention, spend a week noticing what your child concentrates on when nobody is directing them. If there is anything at all — drawing, stacking, digging, a particular toy — the machinery works. The task is to borrow that capacity more often, not to build it from nothing.
</Callout>

## What shortens attention

Most short sessions are explained by something on this list rather than by the child:

- **Tiredness or hunger.** The two biggest, and the easiest to fix by moving the time.
- **The task is too hard** — or too easy. Both produce the same walking-away.
- **It isn't clear when it ends.** "Let's do some letters" has no finish line. "Three letters, then we're done" does.
- **Too much on the table.** Four activities within reach means four decisions a minute.
- **Noise and movement.** A television on in the background costs more attention than most adults realise.
- **Being interrupted.** Including by us — questions, corrections, photographs.

## What lengthens it

- **Let them choose** within limits: *"letters or numbers today?"*
- **Sit with them.** A child alone at a table gives up far sooner than one with an adult alongside.
- **Set a visible end point.** Three pages, two levels, until the timer runs out.
- **Match the difficulty.** Slightly challenging and achievable is the zone; everything else loses them.
- **Clear the space.** One activity out at a time.
- **Stop before they do.** Ending while a child still has appetite is the single best way to lengthen the next session.

## Screens are a special case

A child can watch a screen for an hour and still seem unable to attend to a book for five minutes. That is not a contradiction, and it is not proof of a long attention span.

Fast-paced video holds attention from the outside — new thing, new thing, new thing — rather than requiring the child to sustain it from the inside. It is closer to being held than to concentrating.

This is why **what** is on the screen matters more than how long: something that stops and waits for a child to answer asks for real attention; something that plays on regardless does not. The [screen time guide](/blog/how-much-screen-time-for-toddlers) covers that difference in more detail.

## A five-minute learning moment that works

- Pick a time that is not just before a meal or a nap
- Clear everything else off the table
- Say what it is and when it ends: *"two pages, then we'll go outside"*
- Sit with them
- Stop at the stated end, even if it is going well
- Say what they did, not how clever they are: *"you finished both pages"*

Five minutes, four times a week, beats twenty minutes once — for attention as much as for learning.

## What is worth mentioning to a professional

Attention varies hugely, and most short attention is ordinary. It is worth raising with a teacher, GP or health visitor if you see:

- No sustained attention to **anything**, including things your child chose
- Attention that is much shorter than other children the same age, across settings — home, nursery, relatives' houses
- Constant movement that stops them doing things they want to do
- Difficulty that is getting worse rather than slowly better
- A teacher raising it independently

Age matters a lot here: concerns about a three-year-old's attention are usually about expectations, while the same pattern at six and in a classroom is worth a conversation.
