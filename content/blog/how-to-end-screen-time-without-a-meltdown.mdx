For many families, the hardest part of screen time is not how much there is. It is the moment it ends. The phone goes away, and a calm child becomes a crying, bargaining, floor-flopping one in about four seconds.

That reaction is common, it is not a sign that something is wrong with your child, and most of it can be headed off before it starts.

## The short answer

Give a warning your child can actually understand, end at a natural stopping point rather than mid-activity, have the next thing ready, and keep the ending the same every day. When a meltdown happens anyway, stay calm, name the feeling, and hold the line without a lecture.

## Why it's so hard for them

Three things combine at that moment:

- **They were absorbed.** A screen holds attention very completely. Being pulled out of that is jarring for anyone — adults included.
- **They have no sense of time.** "Five more minutes" means almost nothing to a three-year-old. The end always feels sudden to them, even when you warned them.
- **The next thing is less exciting.** Switching from a bright, responsive screen to "time for your bath" is a steep drop.

None of these are about defiance. They are about a young brain changing gear, which is a skill children are still building.

## Warn them in units they understand

Replace minutes with something concrete.

- **"Two more levels, then we stop."**
- **"When this story ends, it's time for dinner."**
- **"One more song."**

A child can see a level finish or a story end. They cannot see five minutes pass. Count down out loud as you go — *"that's one level done, one more"* — so the ending is a thing they watched approaching rather than a surprise.

For younger children, a visual timer helps more than a spoken warning, because they can watch the time shrink.

## Stop at a natural end point

Ending in the middle of something is the single biggest trigger. Nobody wants to stop halfway through a game or a video.

Choose content that has built-in endings — a level, an episode, a story — and stop at one of those. If what they are watching or playing never ends on its own, the stopping point has to be imposed from outside, and that is always harder.

<Callout title="Let them press the button">
Hand over control of the ending. "Can you close it for me?" or "You switch it off when this level finishes." A child who ends the session themselves feels in charge of it, and there is far less to fight against.
</Callout>

## Use "when–then", not threats

*"When the level is done, then we'll go to the park"* works much better than *"if you don't stop now, no phone tomorrow."*

The first tells a child what happens next and gives them something to move towards. The second makes the moment of stopping feel like a punishment, which is exactly the feeling you are trying to avoid.

## Have the next thing ready

A gap after the screen is where the meltdown lives. Have something to go straight into:

- A snack, handed over as the screen goes away
- A job they like — pressing the lift button, feeding the dog
- A quick game with you — even one of [the no-toys games](/blog/learning-games-to-play-anywhere-no-toys) works
- Going outside

The more appealing the next thing, the easier the switch. It doesn't have to be exciting — it just has to exist.

## Make it the same every day

Predictability is powerful. If screen time always ends the same way — the same warning, the same stopping point, the same next thing — it becomes a routine rather than a negotiation.

Children push hardest at limits that move. A rule that is sometimes firm and sometimes gives in to crying teaches that crying sometimes works, which makes every ending a test.

## When the meltdown happens anyway

Sometimes it will, especially when your child is tired or hungry. In the moment:

1. **Stay calm and close.** Your calm is the thing they borrow.
2. **Name the feeling.** *"You're sad it's finished. You were really enjoying that."*
3. **Hold the limit.** *"It's still finished. We can play again tomorrow."*
4. **Skip the lecture.** Explanations don't land mid-tears. Talk about it later, if at all.
5. **Don't give the screen back** to stop the crying. It works once and makes every future ending harder.

Once they have calmed down, move on warmly. There's no need to revisit it.

## Watch what the screen is doing for them

If a screen has become the only thing that settles your child, endings will always be hard, because you are taking away their main calming tool. It is worth building a few others — a cuddle, a song, a walk — so the screen is one option among several rather than the only one.

And if meltdowns at screen time are frequent, intense and getting worse, it can help to cut back or change the kind of content for a while. A calmer, more interactive activity usually ends more easily than fast, endless video. The [screen time guide](/blog/how-much-screen-time-for-toddlers) covers the difference.

## A simple routine to try

- Agree the length before it starts: *"Two levels today."*
- Count down as each one ends
- Stop at the end of a level, not in the middle
- Let your child close it
- Go straight into the next thing
- Keep it the same tomorrow
