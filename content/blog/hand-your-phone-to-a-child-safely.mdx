The worry is rarely the app. It is everything *around* the app — the child tapping out of it, finding the camera roll, opening a browser, or buying something with two accidental presses.

Most of that is fixable with settings that already exist on the phone. It takes about two minutes, once.

## The short answer

Pin the app so your child cannot leave it, require a password for every purchase, decide the end of the session before you start, and take two seconds to notice what else is on that screen. Those four things remove almost every realistic problem.

## 1. Pin the app so they cannot leave it

This is the single most useful setting on the phone and most parents have never heard of it.

Android has a feature usually called **app pinning** or **screen pinning**. When it is on, you lock the phone to one app — swiping, home and back all stop working until you unpin it, usually by holding two buttons together.

The exact location varies by phone and Android version, so rather than following a path that may not match your device, open **Settings**, use the search box at the top, and type **pinning**. It generally sits under Security or Privacy.

Turn it on, and choose the option that asks for your PIN before unpinning. Otherwise a determined five-year-old works it out.

## 2. Require a password for every purchase

In the **Google Play Store**, open the menu, find **Settings → Authentication**, and set purchase verification to require your password or fingerprint **for every purchase** — not just the first in thirty minutes.

This is the setting that prevents the stories you read about: a child tapping through a game and a charge arriving. If you share the device with a child at all, it is worth doing even if you never install games.

<Callout title="Check what is already on the screen">
Before handing the phone over, glance at the home screen. Work chat, photos, your banking app, an open browser tab. Pinning solves this for the session, but if pinning is off for any reason, what is one swipe away matters.
</Callout>

## 3. Decide the ending before you start

Not a setting, but it prevents more trouble than any of them.

Agree what the session is before the phone changes hands: *"two levels, then we stop."* Countable units work far better than minutes for a young child, because they can see a level finish and cannot see five minutes pass.

If you want a timer, most Android phones have **Digital Wellbeing** with per-app timers — the app closes itself, which is sometimes easier than being the one who ends it. Be aware it closes mid-activity, which can cause exactly the meltdown you were avoiding, so a countable unit is usually kinder. [Ending screen time without a meltdown](/blog/how-to-end-screen-time-without-a-meltdown) goes into that properly.

## 4. Sound, and where you are

Turn the volume to something reasonable before you hand it over, not after.

For learning apps specifically, do not mute. The whole point of tap-to-hear is the audio — a child learning letter sounds or the Varnamala with the sound off is getting about half of it. Headphones are worth having for journeys and waiting rooms.

## 5. For older children, consider a separate space

If your child is using a device regularly — five and up, say — it is worth more than ad-hoc pinning:

- **A separate user profile** on a shared tablet, with only their apps on it
- **Google Family Link**, which lets you approve apps, set limits and see what is being used
- **Kids Space**, available on some Android devices, which provides a child-only home screen

These are more setup than pinning, and worth it only once a device is being used often enough to justify it.

## A two-minute checklist

Before the phone goes over:

1. **Pinning on**, with PIN required to unpin
2. **Play purchases** set to require authentication every time
3. **Volume** set, headphones if needed
4. **Ending agreed** — two levels, one story, one game
5. **Glance at the home screen**

Then pin the app and hand it over.

## What this does not solve

Device settings control the phone. They say nothing about whether the app itself is any good, and that is the bigger question.

An app can be perfectly pinned and still show a full-screen video ad between every level, or lock the content your child was enjoying behind a subscription, or react to a wrong answer with a noise that makes a four-year-old feel bad. Those are properties of the app, and worth checking before installing rather than after — the [seven-point checklist](/blog/how-to-choose-a-learning-app-for-kids) covers how, and [what "free" actually means](/blog/free-learning-apps-for-kids-what-free-really-means) covers the pricing side.

The two work together. Settings make the device safe; choosing well makes the time worth something.

## One more thing worth knowing

An app that works fully offline keeps working in flight mode, which is useful well beyond flights — on a train, in a basement, in a waiting room with terrible signal, or any time you want a session with nothing else arriving.

It is also one of the few genuinely reliable tests of a learning app. Turn on flight mode before you hand the phone over; if the app still teaches, it was built to be used rather than streamed.
