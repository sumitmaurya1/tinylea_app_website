Search "learning app for kids" and you get thousands of results, nearly all of them claiming to teach the alphabet. Most are games with a letter painted on them. A few are genuinely good. The store listing will not tell you which is which, because every listing says the same things.

Here are seven questions that actually separate them. They take about two minutes to check, and they work on any app — including ours.

## 1. Does it work without Wi-Fi?

Turn on aeroplane mode and open the app. If it shows a spinner or an error, it is not a learning app you can rely on — it is a website in a wrapper.

This matters more than it sounds. The moments you most want a quiet, occupied child are the moments with the worst signal: a flight, a car journey, a waiting room, a relative's house with one bar. An app that needs a connection is unavailable in exactly those moments.

## 2. What happens when your child taps an ad?

Free apps are usually ad-supported, and that is fine. What matters is what the ads *do*.

Tap one deliberately and watch. On a properly configured kids' app it should be clearly marked, easy to close, and it must not open a store page, a browser, or a game with a purchase button. Google's Families policy requires ads in child-directed apps to be age-appropriate and clearly labelled — apps that ignore this tend to be the ones showing full-screen video ads between every level.

<Callout title="The two-tap test">
Hand the phone to your child and watch their first two minutes without helping. If they need you to read something, tap something precise, or get out of a screen, the app was designed for you, not for them.
</Callout>

## 3. Can a non-reader use it alone?

A four-year-old cannot read "Settings", "Continue", or "Choose a level". If the navigation depends on reading, your child depends on you, and the app stops being something they can pick up on their own.

Look for big targets, one idea per screen, and icons that mean something without a caption. If every screen needs your finger, the app has failed at the one job that makes it useful.

## 4. Is there a parent gate?

Anything that leads out of the play area — settings, external links, anything involving money — should sit behind a check a small child cannot pass. Usually that is a short maths problem or a press-and-hold.

Open the settings and see what it takes to get there. If a three-year-old can reach a purchase screen by tapping randomly, that is not an oversight you should forgive.

## 5. Does it teach in the language you actually speak at home?

Most apps stop at English. If your family speaks Hindi or Marathi at home, an English-only app quietly teaches your child that the language they hear at dinner is not the one that counts as learning.

Check whether the second language is a real topic with its own audio and its own place in the games, or a token tab with a few pictures. The [Hindi Varnamala](/blog/learn-hindi-for-kids-varnamala-guide) deserves the same treatment as the alphabet — tap-to-hear letters, example words, real practice.

## 6. Does the difficulty actually go up?

Play level 1, then skip ahead as far as you can. If level 40 is the same task with a different picture, your child will be bored inside a fortnight and you will have taught them that learning apps are a waste of time.

Good progression is felt, not advertised: more on screen, less time, closer choices. A hundred levels of the same thing is one level, printed a hundred times.

## 7. What happens when they get it wrong?

This is the one most parents never check, and it is the one that shapes how your child feels about being wrong.

Watch a deliberate mistake. The app should show the right answer calmly and move on. What you do not want is a buzzer, a sad noise, a crying character, or a score that visibly drops. A four-year-old does not experience that as feedback. They experience it as failing, and children who feel they are failing stop volunteering to play.

## A two-minute test before you commit

Do all of this before you hand the phone over:

1. **Aeroplane mode** — does it still open and teach?
2. **Tap an ad** — clearly marked, easy to close, goes nowhere it should not?
3. **Open settings** — is there a parent gate?
4. **Get one wrong on purpose** — is the response calm?
5. **Hand it to your child** — can they get anywhere without you?

An app that passes all five is rarer than the store listings suggest.
