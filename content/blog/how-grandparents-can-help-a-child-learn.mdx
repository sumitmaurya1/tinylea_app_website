The weekly video call goes the same way in a lot of families. The phone is held up, the child is told to say hello, they mumble something and run off, and the adults talk to each other for twenty minutes.

Everyone is disappointed and nobody says so.

Grandparents are one of the most underused resources in early learning, and the fix is small: give the call a job.

## The short answer

Children do not perform on demand, so a call built around *"say hello to Nani"* will fail most weeks. A call built around a shared activity — counting something, reading a story, naming photographs — works almost every time, because the child has something to do rather than something to be.

## What grandparents are unusually good at

Not teaching, exactly. Four other things:

- **Time.** The thing parents have least of. A grandparent will happily hear the same story four times.
- **Patience without an agenda.** No one is late for school in a grandparent's conversation.
- **Repetition.** The engine of early learning, and tedious for parents.
- **The language.** Often the strongest Hindi or Marathi in the family, and always the most natural.

None of that requires being good with children's education. It requires something to do.

## Ten things to do on a video call

Pick one. Five minutes each is plenty.

1. **Count something together** — steps, bananas, people in the room. Let the child lead.
2. **I spy**, using the first *sound* of a word rather than the letter name.
3. **Read one short story.** Ask the child what happens next before turning the page.
4. **Name old photographs.** Who is this? Where was this? Children love this one.
5. **Teach a rhyme**, one line a week, until the child can do it alone.
6. **Spell their name together**, one letter at a time.
7. **Show and tell** — the child fetches three things and explains them.
8. **Describe and guess.** "I am thinking of something round and red."
9. **Sing.** Songs carry vocabulary and rhythm with no effort from anyone.
10. **Ask about the day in the home language**, and wait for the answer.

<Callout title="Give the grandparent the plan, not the child">
Send the grandparent one line before the call: "this week we are on the letter M" or "she can count to twelve now". A grandparent who knows what is being worked on will reinforce it naturally. A grandparent with no information will default to asking how school was.
</Callout>

## Recorded beats live more often than you would think

Time zones defeat a lot of families. A grandparent in India and a grandchild in Toronto have perhaps one workable hour a day, usually the worst hour of the child's.

Voice notes solve this. Ask grandparents to record:

- A story, in the home language
- A rhyme or a song
- Counting one to ten
- Just talking — what they did today, what the weather is doing

The child can play them at a good moment, repeatedly, which live calls never allow. For language especially, hearing more than two adult voices matters: children abroad often conclude the home language is a thing their parents do, and more voices quietly correct that.

## The language is the big one

If you are raising children outside India, this is where a grandparent is worth more than any class or app.

A child learns a language they have a reason to use, and a grandparent who does not speak much English is the most honest reason available. Not a rule from parents, not homework — a person they actually want to talk to.

That is why a short weekly call where the only language is Hindi or Marathi does more than an hour of instruction. [Teaching a home language abroad](/blog/teaching-hindi-marathi-abroad-nri-families) covers how to build that into a routine, and [the comparison of classes, tutors and apps](/blog/hindi-marathi-classes-for-kids-abroad) is honest about where each one actually helps.

## Same thing, two places

A quiet trick that works well: the child and the grandparent do the *same* activity separately during the week, then compare.

Both learn the same rhyme. Both count the same thing. Both look at the same five pictures. The call then has a natural subject, and the child arrives with something to say rather than something to be asked.

In the app, a "Star of the Day" card can be sent on to a grandparent after a good session — a shareable progress card you can add the child's name to. A small thing, and children take it seriously.

## For grandparents who are not comfortable with phones

Keep it to one app and one button, set up by someone else. Audio-only calls are fine — for language, they are nearly as good, and they remove the awkwardness of being looked at.

And if grandparents cannot read on a screen, nothing above requires it. Counting, rhymes, songs, stories told from memory and old photographs all work without a single written word.

## What to avoid

- **Quizzing.** *"What's this? And this? What's two plus two?"* turns a call into a test and shortens the next one.
- **Comparing with cousins.** Fast route to a child who does not want to come to the phone.
- **Long calls.** Five good minutes beats twenty-five minutes of the child drifting away.
- **Correcting the child's language.** Especially the home language. A child corrected twice will answer in English the third time.

## If grandparents live nearby

Everything above still applies, with one addition: give them a **standing job**. The bedtime story, the walk to the shop, counting the washing. A regular role beats occasional enthusiasm, and it is far easier for everyone to keep going.

Learning that happens with someone who loves them is remembered differently from learning that happens at a table.
