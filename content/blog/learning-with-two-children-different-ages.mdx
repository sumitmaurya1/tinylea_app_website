Most advice about learning at home quietly assumes one child. In a lot of houses there are two, three years apart, wanting different things at the same moment — and one adult.

Running two separate sessions is the obvious answer and the one that collapses by Wednesday. Here is what works instead.

## The short answer

Run one activity with two jobs in it rather than two activities. Give the older child a role that is genuinely harder, not just more of the same, and give the younger one something they can succeed at without help. Split them only when one is being held back or embarrassed.

## One activity, two jobs

The trick is to pick something with a natural range in it, then hand each child a different part.

**Counting.** The younger one counts out five spoons. The older one works out how many more are needed for everyone, or counts in twos.

**Letters.** The younger one finds every **M** on the page. The older one reads the words those **M**s are in.

**Shapes.** The younger one names them. The older one sorts them by a rule and says what the rule was.

**A story.** The younger one predicts what happens next. The older one retells it afterwards in order.

**Colours.** The younger one finds three red things. The older one finds something that is *almost* red and explains the difference.

Same table, same subject, same ten minutes — two genuinely different demands.

## Let the older one teach, carefully

Explaining something is one of the best ways to consolidate it, and a six-year-old teaching a three-year-old is usually doing real work.

Use it deliberately: *"can you show her how you do that one?"* The older child has to slow down, sequence it and put it into words, which is harder than doing it.

The limit matters though. A child who is always the teacher stops getting taught, and starts to resent the arrangement. Keep it to a minute or two, and make sure they get something that stretches them in the same session.

<Callout title="Watch who is talking">
If the older child answers everything, the younger one quietly stops trying — there is no point. Try directing questions by name, and giving the younger child first go at the easy ones. The older child can be asked to wait, which is itself a useful thing to practise.
</Callout>

## Protect the younger one from always losing

In anything competitive, the three-year-old loses to the six-year-old every time, and learns something unhelpful from that.

Options that work:

- **Handicaps**, stated openly: *"she gets to start, you have to do two."*
- **Team games** against a parent rather than each other.
- **Different win conditions.** The older one is racing their own last score; the younger one is trying to finish.
- **Nobody wins.** Plenty of activities do not need a winner at all.

## Protect the older one too

The other failure is subtler. Activities get pitched at the younger child because that keeps the peace, and the older one coasts through months of things they mastered a year ago.

Signs of it: boredom, silliness, suddenly refusing to join in. That is usually not attitude. It is a child who has worked out the session has nothing in it for them.

They need at least one thing per session that they cannot already do.

## Sharing one screen

If an app is part of it, the same principle applies — take turns by activity rather than by clock.

Turns that end on their own are far easier to manage than turns that end because you said so: one level each, or one topic each. A child who finishes a level and hands over is not being interrupted. A child stopped halfway through is.

It is also fine for them to be on completely different topics. A three-year-old on colours and a six-year-old on phonics are using the same app and learning different things, which is the point.

## A ten-minute shape

- **2 minutes together** — a song, counting, something everyone can do
- **3 minutes** — younger child's task, older child helping or doing their own
- **3 minutes** — older child's harder task, younger child watching or playing alongside
- **2 minutes together** — a game, something silly, finish well

The watching is not wasted time. Younger siblings pick up a surprising amount from being near an older child's work, which is part of why second children often arrive at school knowing things nobody taught them.

## When to split them up

Keep them together by default, but separate when:

- The younger one is **embarrassed** at how much easier it is for their sibling
- The older one is being **held back** session after session
- They cannot be in the same room without it becoming a fight
- One is learning something the other finds boring — early reading especially

Ten minutes each, at different times of day, beats twenty minutes of managing a quarrel. Bath time, bedtime, the school run and the time one is napping are the usual slots.

## If it mostly turns into fighting

Shorten it. Most sibling learning sessions fail because they are too long, not because the children cannot cooperate.

Five good minutes, finished while everyone is still cheerful, is worth far more than twenty that end in tears — the same rule that applies [when a child says they cannot do something](/blog/when-your-child-says-i-cant-do-it).
