Every year, in the months before school starts, the same worry goes around: *should she know all her letters by now?* Usually the question arrives after a conversation with another parent whose child apparently reads already.

Readiness is a range, not a line. Children arrive at school knowing wildly different things and mostly even out within a year. What follows is what genuinely helps on day one — and, just as usefully, what does not matter nearly as much as it feels like it does.

## What "ready" actually means

School readiness is less about academic knowledge than most parents expect. A teacher with thirty children needs each of them to manage a coat, sit for a story, ask for the toilet, and recover from a small disappointment without the day ending.

The letters and numbers matter too. They are just further down the list than the anxiety suggests.

## Letters: recognising beats reciting

Singing the alphabet is a party trick. It is a sequence learned as one long word, and a child who sings it fluently may still not pick out **S** on a page.

What is genuinely useful before school:

- Recognises the letters of their **own name**, and can spot them elsewhere
- Knows that letters are different from numbers and from pictures
- Has heard that letters make sounds, even if they cannot do all of them

That is it. Not all twenty-six, not upper and lower case, not sounds for every letter. If you want a place to start, [start with their name](/blog/how-to-teach-alphabet-to-toddlers) rather than with A.

## Numbers: counting is not the same as quantity

Most four-year-olds can count to twenty. Far fewer can hand you four spoons.

Counting aloud is sequence memory. Understanding that "four" *is* a quantity — that it stays four whether the objects are spread out or bunched up — is the part school builds on. Check it in ten seconds: put out five things and ask for three of them.

<Callout title="The gap worth closing">
If your child can count to fifty but cannot fetch four spoons, spend your time on small quantities of real objects rather than on counting higher. Counting to a hundred is a nice trick; knowing what four means is the foundation.
</Callout>

Before school, comfortable counting to ten with one-to-one matching — one number per object, touched as they go — is worth more than reciting to a hundred. Higher counting can come [later, and it will come quickly](/blog/teaching-kids-to-count-1-to-100).

## Shapes, colours and sorting

These get skipped because they feel too easy, but sorting is the beginning of mathematical thinking: noticing a property, grouping by it, explaining the rule.

Useful before school: names most common colours, recognises circle, square and triangle, and can sort a pile of mixed objects by one property and tell you why. The last part — saying *why* — is the bit that matters.

## Listening, and two-step instructions

This is the one teachers actually raise at parents' evenings.

"Put your shoes on and bring me your bag" is two instructions in one sentence. A child who can hold both, in order, without a reminder is a child who can follow a classroom morning. It is a skill, and it improves with practice.

Practise it the cheap way: give two-step instructions at home, in the ordinary run of the day, and resist the urge to repeat yourself immediately.

## Pencil grip and attention span

Fine motor control develops on its own schedule, and pushing it early achieves very little. Threading, playdough, tearing paper and picking up small objects build the same muscles as handwriting practice, without the frustration.

On attention: five to ten minutes on one activity is a reasonable expectation at four. Not thirty. If your child leaves an activity after eight minutes, that is not a problem to fix.

## The things that matter more than any of the above

Ask a reception teacher what they wish children arrived with, and the answer is rarely academic:

1. **Managing a coat, shoes and a lunchbox** with some independence
2. **Asking an adult for help** — out loud, to a stranger
3. **Using the toilet** and washing hands unprompted
4. **Separating from you** without a long unravelling
5. **Taking turns** and surviving losing a game

Every one of these is more predictive of a smooth first term than knowing the alphabet. If you have limited energy, spend it here.

## A ten-minute-a-day plan for the last term

Short and consistent beats long and occasional. A workable shape:

- **3 minutes** on letters — their name first, then a few distinct shapes like O, S, T, X
- **3 minutes** on quantity — counting real objects to ten, fetching exact amounts
- **2 minutes** on shapes or sorting — with a spoken reason
- **2 minutes** of listening practice — one two-step instruction, done properly

Ten minutes. Most days, not all. Stop before they want to stop, so it stays something they will agree to tomorrow.

## When to stop worrying, and when to ask

Stop worrying about: not knowing all the letters, not reading, not writing their name neatly, counting only to twelve, holding a pencil oddly at four.

Worth raising with your health visitor, GP or their nursery: not responding to their own name, very limited speech compared with children the same age, no interest in other children, or losing a skill they previously had. Those are conversations to have early, and the people who see many children are the right people to have them with.

Everything else is a range. Your child is somewhere in it, and so is everyone else's.
