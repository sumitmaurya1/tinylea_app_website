A child who knows all twenty-six letter names can still be unable to read the word *cat*. This surprises parents, and it is the single most common place early reading stalls.

Letter names and letter sounds are different pieces of knowledge. Reading runs on the sounds.

## Names and sounds are not the same thing

The letter **B** has a name — "bee" — and a sound, the one at the start of *ball*. The name is a label. The sound is the thing you actually use to read.

Try reading *cat* with names: "see-ay-tee". It produces nothing. Now with sounds: /k/ /a/ /t/. That is a word.

Both are worth knowing eventually. But if you are choosing where to spend a ten-minute session with a four-year-old, sounds do more work.

## The mistake almost every parent makes

Say the sound of **B** out loud right now. Most people say *"buh"*.

That extra "uh" is the problem. It is a vowel you have added, and the child now has to un-learn it. Watch what it does to a three-letter word:

- With the vowel: "cuh — a — tuh" → *cuhatuh*
- Without: /k/ — /a/ — /t/ → *cat*

The first one does not blend into anything. Children who cannot blend are very often children who were taught sounds with a vowel bolted on.

<Callout title="How to check yourself">
Say the sound and stop your mouth immediately. /b/ is a tiny puff, not a syllable. If you can sing it, hold it, or hear "uh" at the end, trim it. This takes a few tries and then it sticks.
</Callout>

Some sounds can be stretched — /s/, /m/, /f/, /l/, /n/ — and those are much easier for a beginner because you can hold them while the child catches up. Others stop dead: /b/, /t/, /k/, /p/, /d/. Teach the stretchy ones first and blending gets easier by itself.

## Which sounds to teach first

Not A to Z. Alphabetical order is an adult convenience that delays the moment a child can read an actual word.

The widely used first set is **s, a, t, p, i, n** — six sounds chosen because they combine into a surprising number of real words: *sat, tap, pin, nip, tin, pit, sit, pan*. A child who has six sounds and can blend them is reading. A child who has twenty-six and cannot blend is not.

After that, useful early additions: **m, d, g, o, c, k**.

## Blending is the actual skill

Knowing sounds is the easy half. Blending — running them together fast enough that a word appears — is the part that takes time, and it usually arrives suddenly rather than gradually.

What helps:

1. **Start with two sounds**, not three. *at, in, up, on.*
2. **Say it fast, then faster.** Stretch the first sound and run straight into the next: *ssssss-at.* The pause between sounds is what stops it working.
3. **Let them hear the word before they read it.** Say the sounds, ask what word it is, no letters in sight. Blending is a listening skill first.
4. **Use real words.** *Nonsense* words have a place in school phonics screening, but at home a real word gives the child the reward of understanding.

Expect this to take weeks and to look like no progress right up until it clicks.

## Do not wait for all 26 letters

The instinct is to finish the alphabet and then start reading. It is the wrong order, and it costs children the motivation that comes from reading something.

Six sounds is enough to start. Add more while blending is already underway. If your child is still working on recognising letters at all, [start with the letters in their name](/blog/how-to-teach-alphabet-to-toddlers) and come back to this.

## What comes after single sounds

Once single letters and blending are comfortable, English starts making its excuses:

- **Digraphs** — two letters, one sound: *sh, ch, th, ck, ng*
- **Long vowels** — the *a* in *cake* is not the *a* in *cat*
- **Tricky words** — *the, said, was, one.* These do not follow the rules and are learned by sight

None of this is urgent at four or five. Introduce digraphs only when blending three sounds is easy.

## Ten minutes, four days a week

- **2 minutes** — review sounds already known, fast, no hesitation allowed
- **3 minutes** — one new sound: say it, find it, write it in the air
- **3 minutes** — blend two or three sounds aloud, no letters
- **2 minutes** — read two or three real words on paper

Stop while they still want to go on. Four short sessions beat one long one, every time.

## If it is not clicking

Blending is genuinely hard and arrives at very different ages. Before worrying, check the two most common causes: sounds being said with an extra vowel, and moving to three sounds before two are solid.

If a child is five or older, has had consistent practice, and still cannot blend two sounds after several months, mention it to their teacher. Ask specifically about phonological awareness — hearing and manipulating sounds — since that is usually where the difficulty sits, not with the letters themselves.
