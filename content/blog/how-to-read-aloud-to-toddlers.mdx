Most parents read aloud the way they were read to: open the book, read the words, turn the page, finish, lights out. That is lovely, and it is not nothing. But it leaves most of the value on the table.

The version that builds the most language is less like a performance and more like a conversation — and it is easier than it sounds.

## The short answer

Read *with* your child rather than *to* them. Pause, ask questions, let them finish familiar lines, and follow what catches their attention rather than the text. Ten minutes of this, most days, from the time they can sit on your lap, is one of the most useful things you can do for early language.

## The words on the page are the smallest part

A picture book has perhaps a hundred words. The conversation around it can easily have five hundred — and those words are about things your child is looking at, right now, which is exactly when words stick.

*"Look, the dog is hiding. Where do you think he went? Under the bed? Let's turn and see."* None of that is in the book. All of it is language your child is hearing in context.

## Turn the book into a conversation

Researchers who study this call it *dialogic reading*. The core loop is short enough to remember:

1. **Prompt** — ask something about the page
2. **Respond** to what they say, warmly
3. **Expand** it — add a little to their answer
4. **Repeat** — ask again so they can use the fuller version

In practice:

> *"What's that?"* — *"Dog."* — *"Yes, a big brown dog. He's running. What's the dog doing?"* — *"Dog running."*

Your child said two words. Then three. Nobody corrected anybody.

## Five kinds of question

Vary the prompts and you stretch different skills:

- **Finish the line** — pause before a rhyme or a repeated phrase and let them fill it: *"Brown bear, brown bear, what do you…"*
- **Remember** — *"What happened to the cat at the start?"*
- **Open questions** — *"What's happening on this page?"* Better than yes/no.
- **Who, what, where** — *"Where is the ball?"* Point if they need help.
- **Link it to their life** — *"We saw a cow like that at Nani's house, didn't we?"*

The last one is the most powerful and the most skipped. Connecting a story to something a child actually lived is what makes it matter to them.

<Callout title="Match the question to the child">
For under-twos, stick to pointing and naming: *"Where's the duck?"* Two- and three-year-olds can handle what and where. Save *"why do you think…?"* for fours and fives — asking it too early just makes reading feel like a test.
</Callout>

## Reading the same book forty times is fine

Children ask for the same book again and again, and many parents quietly hide it. Don't.

Repetition is how a child moves from hearing a story to knowing it — and once they know it, they start to predict, join in and eventually "read" it back to you from memory. That is not a trick. It is an early reading skill. Use the familiarity: pause more, and let them do more of the telling each time.

## You don't have to finish the book

If your child wants to stay on one page for five minutes talking about a tractor, stay on the page. If they want to skip to the end, skip. The book is a prompt for talking, not a task to complete.

The one thing worth avoiding is pushing on when they have clearly lost interest. A session that ends while they still want more is the one they will ask for tomorrow.

## Read in the language you speak at home

If your family speaks Hindi or Marathi, read in it — or tell the story in it while looking at an English book. Children build language on whatever they hear most richly, and a story told fluently in your strongest language is worth more than one read stiffly in your weaker one.

[Two languages will not confuse them](/blog/will-two-languages-confuse-my-child). If anything, stories are one of the best ways to keep a home language alive.

## Point at the words, sometimes

Once your child is three or four, occasionally run your finger under the words as you read. It shows them, without any lesson, that print goes left to right, that words are separate, and that the black marks are what you are saying.

Keep it light. You are not teaching reading yet — you are showing that print carries meaning. The sounds come later, and [phonics](/blog/how-to-teach-phonics-at-home) takes over from there.

## When they won't sit still

Many toddlers will not sit for a whole book, and that is normal rather than a sign they dislike reading.

- **Let them move.** A child walking around the room is often still listening.
- **Let them hold the book** and turn the pages, even out of order.
- **Choose short books** with big pictures and very few words per page.
- **Read at a calm time**, not straight after something exciting.
- **Two minutes counts.** Several short sessions beat one long struggle.

## A simple routine

- Pick a regular slot — bedtime is the classic for a reason
- Let your child choose the book, even if it is the same one again
- Ask one or two questions per page, no more
- Link one thing to their own life
- Stop while it is still fun
