"100+ learning games" appears on almost every app listing in the children's category, and it tells you nothing. A hundred levels of the same tapping exercise is one game, printed a hundred times.

So here is the honest version of ours: eight mini-games, what a child actually does in each, the skill it builds, and something you can do at home to practise the same thing without a screen.

## How the games fit together

The eight games repeat across **100 levels** arranged as a single winding path. There is no menu to get lost in — your child plays the next level, and that is the whole navigation.

Each level is scored out of three stars, and **two stars unlock the next one**. That threshold matters: it means a child moves on once they have genuinely got it, rather than after one lucky attempt, but it doesn't demand perfection before they can progress.

Difficulty rises across the hundred levels — more on screen, less time, closer choices — so the same game feels different at level 60 than at level 6.

## The eight games

### 1. Match the Shapes

**What they do:** drag each shape to the outline that fits it.

**Builds:** shape recognition.

**At home:** ask *"how many corners?"* rather than *"what shape is it?"* Counting sides and corners is what lets a child recognise a triangle when it's rotated or stretched — [the thing most shape books get wrong](/blog/how-to-teach-shapes-to-toddlers).

### 2. Pop the Word

**What they do:** spot and pop the bubble holding the right word.

**Builds:** vocabulary.

**At home:** play I-spy using the first *sound* of a word rather than the letter name — "/b/" not "bee". It's the same spot-the-right-one skill, and it feeds straight into reading.

### 3. Math Runner

**What they do:** run, jump and pick the answer before the gap closes.

**Builds:** early math.

**At home:** ask quick sums about real things — two rotis plus one more. The time pressure in the game rewards recall rather than counting on fingers, and everyday practice is what builds that.

### 4. Sentence Rocket

**What they do:** put words in the right order to launch the rocket.

**Builds:** sentence building.

**At home:** say a sentence with the words jumbled — *"apples love I"* — and let your child fix it. Deliberately getting one wrong yourself is usually good for a laugh.

### 5. Ocean Clean-up

**What they do:** sort what belongs in the sea from what doesn't.

**Builds:** sorting and focus.

**At home:** sort anything — socks by colour, spoons from forks — and ask your child to say *why* they sorted it that way. Explaining the rule is the part that builds the thinking.

### 6. Geo Explorer

**What they do:** explore places and match them to their clues.

**Builds:** world knowledge.

**At home:** give directions rather than pointing: *"two steps forward, then turn left."* Following a described route is the same skill in the real world.

### 7. Weather Dressing

**What they do:** dress the character for the weather on screen.

**Builds:** reasoning.

**At home:** let your child choose what to wear based on the weather outside, and ask why. It's a genuine decision with a visible reason, which is exactly what the game practises.

### 8. Synonym Dino Feed

**What they do:** feed the dinosaur words that mean the same thing.

**Builds:** word meaning.

**At home:** play *"another word for…"* — big, huge, enormous. This is one of the strongest predictors of later reading comprehension, and it costs nothing to practise in a queue.

<Callout title="One game at a time is fine">
If your child wants to replay the same mini-game over and over, let them. Repetition is how the skill moves from effortful to automatic — and a child choosing their own practice is a child who will keep practising.
</Callout>

## What the rewards are actually for

Stars, stickers, collectible prizes, a daily spin-the-wheel gift, a day-streak counter and a shareable "Star of the Day" card.

It's worth being clear-eyed about these: rewards get a young child to come back and try again on a day when they don't feel like it. They are motivation, not learning. The learning is in the levels.

Two of them are deliberately gentle. The streak counter rewards **showing up**, not long sessions — so a child who plays for four minutes keeps their streak. And the Star of the Day card is something to send to a grandparent, which for most children is a bigger reward than anything on screen.

## Playing together beats playing alone

The single biggest upgrade to any of this is you, sitting next to them for a few minutes.

A child playing Sentence Rocket alone practises word order. A child playing it with a parent who says *"read it back to me — does that sound right?"* practises word order **and** has a conversation about it. Same game, much more value.

You don't need to do it every session. Once a day, for five minutes, is plenty.

## Where the games sit alongside the lessons

The games are the practice half of the app. The learn topics — alphabet, the Hindi and Marathi Varnamala, numbers 1–100, colours, shapes, phonics and the rest — are where a child meets something for the first time, tap-to-hear so they know how it sounds.

The games are where it gets used. That order matters: a game is far more enjoyable when a child already half-knows the answer, and much more discouraging when they don't.
