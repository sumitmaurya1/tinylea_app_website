Most families raising children in several languages know what they should be doing. English at school, Hindi with one parent, Marathi or Gujarati with grandparents, everyone reading at bedtime.

Then the week happens, and by Thursday everything has defaulted to whichever language is quickest.

This is a scheduling problem, not a teaching one. [Which language to start and how the scripts differ](/blog/teaching-kids-indian-languages-at-home) is a separate question — this is about the week itself.

## The short answer

Languages grow on hours, so count them honestly, give each language a **role** rather than an equal share, and attach it to something that already happens every day. A language with a slot survives. A language with good intentions does not.

## Count the hours first

A child at an English-medium school gets perhaps thirty-five to forty hours of English a week without anyone planning it. School, friends, signs, television, the playground.

Whatever else you want your child to speak has to come out of the remaining waking hours at home — realistically four or five hours a day, most of it spent on getting out of the house, meals and bedtime.

That is the real competition. Not because English is better, but because it arrives for free. Anything else needs deliberate space.

## Give each language a job, not a percentage

Trying to split the week evenly fails, because the languages are not doing the same work.

A more durable split:

- **English** — school, homework, reading practice, screens
- **Home language** — meals, play, affection, discipline, bedtime stories
- **Third language** — one specific relationship or activity: grandparents, a weekend class, songs

The home language should own the emotional and everyday parts of life. A language used only for instructions loses to the language used for jokes and comfort.

## Attach each language to a routine, not a time

"Twenty minutes of Gujarati daily" collapses in a week. "Gujarati at dinner" survives for years, because dinner already happens.

Anchors that work:

- **Breakfast and dinner** — the most reliable slots in any house
- **The school run** — ten to twenty minutes, every weekday, already happening
- **Bath and bedtime** — calm, repetitive, perfect for stories and songs
- **Sunday morning calls** to grandparents
- **The walk to the shop**

Pick two or three. Attach a language to each. Do not try to fill the rest.

<Callout title="One rule per household member">
The simplest workable pattern is one language per person: Amma speaks Marathi, Papa speaks Hindi, school does English. Children handle this effortlessly and police it better than adults do. The failure mode is adults drifting — if you switch to English when tired, your child learns the language is optional.
</Callout>

## A week that actually works

A realistic shape, not an ideal one:

**Every weekday**
- Breakfast — home language
- School run — home language, or songs
- School — English
- After school — whatever happens, no rules
- Dinner — home language, everyone at the table
- Bedtime — one story, alternating languages by night

**Weekends**
- One call with grandparents, their language only, fifteen minutes
- One longer story or film in the home language
- Ten minutes of letters or numbers — English on one day, the home script on another

That is it. Roughly ten to twelve hours a week of the home language, which is enough to keep it growing, and nothing in it requires a free evening.

## The weakest language needs a protected slot

In almost every multilingual family, one language quietly loses. Usually it is the third — the grandparents' language, or the one only one parent speaks.

If you want to keep it, it needs a slot nobody is allowed to borrow. Fifteen minutes on a Sunday, the same fifteen minutes every week, is worth more than an hour that happens when there is time — because there is never time.

Be honest with yourself about this. Three strong languages beats four half-languages, and choosing deliberately is better than letting the calendar choose for you.

## When they answer in the wrong language

They will. Most children abroad, and plenty in India, understand the home language perfectly and reply in English.

Do not correct, do not insist, and do not pretend you did not understand — children see through that. Carry on in your language, and repeat back what they said in it, so they hear the version they could have used.

This works over months, not conversations. [What to do when a child answers in English](/blog/teaching-hindi-marathi-abroad-nri-families) covers it in more detail.

## A monthly check

Once a month, ask four questions per language:

1. Can my child **ask for something they want** in it?
2. Can they hold a short conversation with someone who speaks only that language?
3. Have they used it this week with **someone other than me**?
4. Do they ever **choose** it — a song, a story, a joke?

Four yeses means it is healthy. Mostly noes means it has become a language they hear rather than one they use, which is the stage before losing it.

## If there are four languages

It is doable, and it needs ruthlessness about roles. Four languages with vague intentions produces a child who is fluent in one and apologetic about three.

Pick two to be strong — usually school English and the main home language — and give each of the others one clear, protected anchor: Sunday calls, a weekend class, a particular grandparent. That is a realistic outcome, and far better than four languages all drifting.

## What not to do

- **Do not switch languages to make a point.** It turns language into discipline.
- **Do not test your child** on which word is which. It makes them self-conscious.
- **Do not drop a language when school gets hard.** It is almost never the cause.
- **Do not aim for perfect.** A child who speaks a language imperfectly still has it.
