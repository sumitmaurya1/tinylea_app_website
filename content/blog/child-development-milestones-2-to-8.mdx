Every parent eventually lies awake wondering whether their child is "on track". The internet does not help: it offers a precise milestone for every month, and another parent's child who apparently hit all of them early.

This guide takes a calmer view. It covers what most children can do at each age from 2 to 8, what actually supports development, and the short list of signs that genuinely deserve a conversation with a professional.

## The short answer

Development happens across five connected areas — language, thinking, movement, social and emotional skills, and self-care — and every child moves through them at their own pace. Milestones describe what *most* children can do by a certain age, not what every child must do. Wide variation is normal. Losing skills a child already had, or being well behind in several areas at once, is what's worth raising early.

## The five areas of development

- **Language** — understanding words, then using them to ask, explain and tell stories
- **Thinking and early learning** — noticing, sorting, counting, remembering, solving small problems
- **Movement** — big movements like running and climbing, and fine control like drawing and buttoning
- **Social and emotional** — playing with others, managing feelings, taking turns
- **Self-care** — eating, dressing, toileting and other everyday independence

They feed each other. A child learning to talk is also learning to ask for help, which changes how they play with other children. So a child who is racing ahead in one area and taking their time in another is completely typical.

## Ages 2 to 3

Most children in this year:

- Move from single words to short two- and three-word phrases, and understand far more than they can say
- Point to and name familiar objects, body parts and people in pictures
- Run, climb, kick a ball and scribble with a crayon
- Play alongside other children more than *with* them
- Want to do things "by myself", even when they can't quite

Their big job this year is language. Talking with them — and [reading together](/blog/how-to-read-aloud-to-toddlers) — does more than anything else.

## Ages 3 to 4

Most children:

- Speak in sentences that familiar adults understand most of the time
- Ask endless "why" questions
- Recognise a few letters, often from their own name, and count a small number of objects
- Know some colours and shapes
- Begin real pretend play and simple games with other children
- Use the toilet in the day, with help

This is when [colours](/blog/how-to-teach-colours-to-toddlers), [shapes](/blog/how-to-teach-shapes-to-toddlers) and first letters begin to click — through play rather than drills.

## Ages 4 to 5

Most children:

- Tell simple stories and talk about things that happened yesterday
- Count ten or so objects reliably and understand "more" and "less"
- Recognise many letters and start linking them to sounds
- Copy a cross and a square, and try writing parts of their name
- Take turns, share with reminders and begin to manage frustration
- Dress themselves with a little help

This is the year most parents start thinking about school. The [readiness checklist](/blog/kindergarten-readiness-checklist) covers what actually matters — and it's less academic than you'd expect.

## Ages 5 to 6

Most children:

- Hold proper conversations and follow instructions with two or three steps
- Blend letter sounds to read short, simple words
- Count to twenty and beyond, and do simple adding with objects
- Write their name and some letters and numbers
- Make friendships and play cooperative games with rules
- Manage most self-care, including toileting and handwashing

## Ages 6 to 8

Most children:

- Read with growing independence, moving from sounding out to recognising words
- Write simple sentences and short stories
- Add and subtract with small numbers and start to understand place value
- Can focus on a task for longer and plan simple projects
- Understand other people's feelings and work through disagreements with less adult help
- Become much more independent at home and school

## Milestones are ranges, not deadlines

These lists describe what *most* children can do around each age. Some reach them months earlier; many reach them months later, and are perfectly healthy.

Try not to compare children — especially siblings or cousins. A child who talks late often catches up quickly, and early walkers are no more likely to be early readers. Children growing up with two or three languages may also spread their words across languages, which can make them look slower in any one. [That is normal too](/blog/will-two-languages-confuse-my-child).

<Callout title="Look at the direction, not the date">
The most useful question is not "should she be doing this by now?" but "is she moving forward?" A child who is steadily gaining skills, even slowly, is usually doing fine. What matters is progress over months, not position on a chart.
</Callout>

## Growing up now

Children today are developing alongside things earlier generations didn't have — especially screens — and in homes that are often multilingual and busy. None of that changes the basics of how children develop. It does change the choices parents face.

The core is the same as it has always been: plenty of talking, play, sleep, time outdoors and time with people who care about them. Screens can be part of a healthy childhood when they are active, limited and don't replace those things. The [screen time guide](/blog/how-much-screen-time-for-toddlers) has more on getting that balance right.

## What actually supports development

The evidence-backed basics are simple and free:

- **Talk constantly** — narrate your day, ask questions, and listen to the answers
- **Read together** every day, even briefly
- **Play** — especially unstructured play, where your child leads
- **Get outside** — running, climbing and exploring build both body and brain
- **Protect sleep** — it underpins attention, mood and learning
- **Keep routines** — predictable days help children feel safe and cope better with change
- **Let them try things themselves**, even when it's slower

## When to talk to a professional

Most differences are simply variation. It is worth speaking to your paediatrician, health visitor or your child's teacher if you notice:

- Your child **loses skills** they previously had — at any age
- Very little speech, or speech that familiar people can't understand, compared with others of the same age
- Not responding to their name, or little interest in other people
- Being well behind in **several areas** at the same time
- A strong gut feeling that something isn't right

You don't need to be sure before you ask. Early support works best, and if everything turns out to be fine, you have lost nothing. If your child is growing up with more than one language, mention it, so they can be assessed fairly.
