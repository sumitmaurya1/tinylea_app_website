It usually arrives from a relative, or from another parent at the school gate: *"Speak only English at home, or you will confuse her."*

It is one of the most persistent pieces of parenting advice in circulation, and it is not supported by the evidence. Here is what actually happens when a child grows up with two or three languages, and what to do about the parts that genuinely are harder.

## The short answer

Growing up with two languages does not confuse children and does not cause language disorders. Bilingual children hit the major milestones — first words, first sentences — within the same normal range as monolingual children. What changes is how their vocabulary is distributed, not how much of it there is.

## Where the worry came from

The advice is old, and it comes from a real observation misread. Bilingual children often know fewer words *in each individual language* than a monolingual child of the same age. Early researchers measured one language, found a smaller number, and concluded there was a deficit.

Count both languages together and the gap closes. A child with four hundred Hindi words and three hundred English words has seven hundred words. She simply does not have all of them in the language you happened to test.

## "But he mixes both languages in one sentence"

Code-switching — *"main ne apna homework kar liya, can I play now?"* — is the thing that worries parents most, and it is the thing that should worry them least.

Mixing is not a sign of confusion. It follows grammatical rules, and children do it in the same places adult bilinguals do. It usually means one of two things:

- The word they want is more available in the other language right now
- They are talking to someone they know speaks both

Children who are genuinely confused do not produce fluent mixed sentences. They produce halting ones.

<Callout title="What to do when they mix">
Nothing corrective. Do not say "in English please" or repeat it back pointedly. Just reply naturally in the language you normally use with them. Consistency from you does far more than correction does.
</Callout>

## Does it delay speech?

Bilingualism is not a cause of speech delay. This matters, because families are routinely told to drop a language when a child is slow to talk — advice that removes a language from a child's life and does nothing for the underlying issue.

If a child has a genuine language difficulty, it shows up in **both** languages. A child who speaks fluently in Marathi and little English is not delayed. She is a child who has had more Marathi.

## The part that is genuinely harder: two scripts

Where bilingual families do face real work is reading and writing, especially when the scripts differ.

English and Devanagari are not variations on a theme. Devanagari is built around the syllable rather than the letter — the vowel attaches to the consonant as a मात्रा rather than sitting beside it. A child who has learned that English letters sit in a line has to learn a genuinely different system, not a translated one.

Two things make it easier:

1. **Do not run them in parallel from scratch.** Get one script comfortable first, then introduce the second. Which one goes first matters less than not starting both in the same month.
2. **Teach by sound, not by list.** [The Varnamala has a structure](/blog/learn-hindi-for-kids-varnamala-guide) — the rows are grouped by where the sound is made in the mouth. Teaching it as a list to be recited throws that away.

For Marathi families, the [Barakhadi](/blog/learn-marathi-for-kids-barakhadi-varnamala) is the same idea taken further, and it rewards patience in exactly the same way.

## What actually helps

- **Pick a pattern and hold it.** One parent one language, or one language at home and the other outside. The specific pattern matters much less than sticking to it.
- **Quantity beats correctness.** A language grows on hours of input. An imperfect hour of Hindi is worth more than a perfect five minutes.
- **Give the home language a job.** A language used only for scolding will lose to the language used for stories and jokes.
- **Let them hear it from more than one person.** Grandparents, video calls, songs. A language spoken by exactly one adult is fragile.

## When to actually seek advice

Bilingualism is not the reason to worry, but some things are worth raising with your GP, health visitor or their nursery:

- Very few words in **either** language by around two
- Not responding to their own name
- Losing words or skills they previously had
- No interest in communicating — pointing, gesturing, eye contact

Ask early if you are unsure, and make sure whoever assesses your child understands they hear more than one language. A child tested only in English can look delayed when they are not.

## The upside nobody mentions

Set against the imagined risk, the actual outcome is that your child gets a second language for free — at the one age when a language costs nothing to acquire, needs no grammar tables, and leaves no accent.

They also get their grandparents. That one is harder to measure and matters more than any of the rest.
