Your child can name every letter. They have known them for months. And they still cannot read *cat*.

This is one of the most common things parents bring to teachers, and it almost always has a specific, findable cause. Knowing the letters is one of three things reading needs, and it is the one most families stop at.

## The short answer

Reading a word requires three separate things: knowing the **sounds** letters make, **blending** those sounds together fast enough to hear a word, and knowing what the word **means**. A child can be perfect at the first and stuck on the second or third. Find out which, and the fix is usually straightforward.

## A five-minute check

Run these four in order. Stop at the first one that is shaky — that is where the problem is.

**1. Sounds, not names.** Point at **m** and ask *"what sound does this make?"* You want *mmm*, not *em*. Try five or six letters.

**2. Blending by ear, no letters involved.** Say the sounds aloud yourself — *"/c/ ... /a/ ... /t/. What word is that?"* The child does not look at anything. They just listen and tell you.

**3. Reading a word.** Write *cat*, *sit*, *pan*. Can they sound it out and say the word?

**4. Meaning.** Say a word they just read and ask what it is. *"What's a pan?"*

Almost every stuck reader fails at step 1 or step 2. That is good news, because both are fixable at home.

## If step 1 is shaky: names instead of sounds

This is the most common cause, and it usually comes from how the letters were taught.

Alphabet songs, letter charts and most nursery material teach **names** — ay, bee, see. Names are almost useless for reading. *See-ay-tee* produces nothing; /c/ /a/ /t/ produces *cat*.

The fix is to teach the sounds for the letters your child already recognises. You are not starting over — they know the shapes, which is most of the work. You are attaching a second thing to each one.

Watch for the extra vowel while you do it. Most adults say "buh" for **b**, and that added *uh* is exactly what makes blending fail later. [The phonics guide](/blog/how-to-teach-phonics-at-home) covers how to trim it.

<Callout title="Stretchy letters first">
Some sounds can be held — /s/, /m/, /f/, /l/, /n/ — and some stop dead: /b/, /t/, /k/, /p/. Beginners blend stretchy sounds far more easily, because you can hold the first one while the child catches up. Start there and blending gets easier on its own.
</Callout>

## If step 2 is shaky: blending

This is the real skill, and the one most children get stuck on. It is also the one that arrives suddenly — weeks of apparently nothing, then it clicks.

What helps:

- **Two sounds before three.** *at, in, up, on.* Three is a big jump.
- **No pause between sounds.** Stretch the first into the second: *ssss-at*. The gap is what breaks it.
- **Ears before eyes.** Keep practising step 2 — you say the sounds, they say the word — with no letters in front of them. Blending is a listening skill first.
- **Real words.** A child who blends *cat* and pictures a cat gets a reward. A nonsense word gives them nothing.

Expect this to take weeks. Practise two minutes a day rather than twenty minutes a week.

## If step 4 is shaky: meaning

Some children decode beautifully and understand nothing. They are reading in the way you could read a Latin word aloud — correct sounds, no comprehension.

This is a vocabulary problem, not a reading one, and it does not get better with more phonics. It gets better with talk, stories, and words used in context. [Building vocabulary](/blog/how-to-build-your-childs-vocabulary) covers the habits that work — the main one being to use the precise word rather than the simple one.

If this is the issue, it matters more than it looks. Decoding carries a child to about seven or eight; after that, comprehension decides everything.

## The words that do not play fair

Even a child blending well will trip on *the, said, was, one, who*. These do not follow the rules, and trying to sound them out teaches a child that sounding out does not work.

Teach them as whole shapes, a few at a time, and say so plainly: *"this one's a tricky word — it just says 'said'."* Children cope with that far better than with a rule that keeps failing.

## The quiet one: how print works

Occasionally the missing piece is more basic. Check that your child knows print runs left to right, that the spaces separate words, and that it is the marks — not the picture — that carry the message.

Run your finger under the words as you read, sometimes. No lesson needed; it teaches itself.

## When it usually clicks, and when to ask

Children typically start blending somewhere between four and six, with an enormous normal range. A five-year-old who is not yet reading is not behind.

Worth a conversation with their teacher if, after several months of regular practice:

- Your child still cannot blend **two** spoken sounds into a word
- They cannot reliably give sounds for letters they see every day
- They know a word on one line and not three lines later
- Reading is causing real distress rather than ordinary effort
- Someone in the family had significant reading difficulty

Ask specifically about **phonological awareness** — hearing and manipulating sounds — because that is usually where the difficulty sits, rather than with the letters themselves.

## The order that works

1. Letter shapes (they already have this)
2. Letter **sounds**
3. Blending by ear
4. Blending with letters in front of them
5. Tricky words, a few at a time
6. Meaning, running through all of it

Most stuck readers are missing exactly one of these, usually number two or three.
