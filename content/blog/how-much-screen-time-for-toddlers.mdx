No parenting topic generates more guilt than this one, and almost none is discussed less usefully. The advice arrives as a number, the number is impossible in a real week, and the parent quietly decides the guidance is not for them.

So here is the number, and then the part that matters more.

## The short answer

The World Health Organization and the American Academy of Pediatrics broadly agree: avoid screens under 18 months apart from video calls, keep it minimal and shared between 18 months and 2 years, and cap it around an hour a day of good-quality content for ages 2 to 5. Beyond 5, both move away from fixed limits toward consistent household rules.

## Why the number is the least useful part

An hour of a child and a parent naming animals together, and an hour of autoplaying videos alone in a dark room, are not the same hour. Counting minutes treats them as though they are.

Most researchers in this area have moved toward what is sometimes called the "three Cs" — **content, context and the child**. Not because the time limit is wrong, but because it is the least informative thing you can know about a child's screen use.

## The three questions worth more than the timer

**What is on the screen?** Something that asks the child to do things — choose, answer, say a word aloud — is different in kind from something that plays at them. Interaction is the dividing line, not the label "educational" in the store listing.

**Who is with them?** Co-viewing is the single most reliable upgrade available. A child watching alone gets input; a child watching with a parent who says *"look, that's a K — like Kabir"* gets a conversation. The screen has not changed. The value has.

**When is it happening?** Screen time that displaces sleep, meals or outdoor play costs something specific. Screen time during the half-hour you need to cook dinner displaces very little.

<Callout title="A more useful question than 'how long?'">
Instead of "how many minutes today?", ask "what did it replace?" Twenty minutes instead of a walk is a different trade from twenty minutes instead of you shouting from the kitchen. Both are fine some days. Only one of them is worth watching.
</Callout>

## Passive and active are not the same thing

If you take one distinction away, take this one.

**Passive** — video that continues without the child, autoplay, background TV. Its defining feature is that nothing happens differently if the child looks away. Background TV is worth singling out: it measurably reduces the amount of talking in a room, and talking is where language comes from.

**Active** — the child taps, answers, chooses, repeats a word out loud. It stops when they stop. The interaction is the point, not decoration around a video.

The hour limit was written with passive viewing in mind. It is a poor fit for ten minutes of a child saying letter sounds back to a screen, and most guidance now says so.

## The rule that is genuinely well-supported

Screens close to bedtime interfere with sleep, and sleep matters more for a young child than almost anything else on this page.

If you keep only one rule, make it this: nothing on a screen in the last hour before bed. It is more consistently supported than any specific daily limit, and it is easier to hold because it is a clear line rather than a running total.

## What "high quality" actually means

The phrase appears in every set of guidance and is almost never defined. In practice:

- It asks the child to **respond**, not just watch
- It **ends** — a natural stopping point instead of an infinite feed
- **No autoplay**, and no design pressure to continue
- Ads, if any, are clearly marked and lead nowhere they should not
- A non-reader can use it **without you** reading the buttons

Those are checkable in about two minutes. If you want the longer version, the [seven-point checklist](/blog/how-to-choose-a-learning-app-for-kids) covers what to look for and how to test it before handing the phone over.

## A plan that survives a real week

- **Pick a slot, not a total.** "After dinner while I clear up" is enforceable. "About an hour" is not.
- **End on a boundary, not a timer.** Finishing a level ends cleanly. An alarm mid-activity starts a fight.
- **Sit with them once a day**, even for five minutes. That is where most of the value is.
- **Keep the last hour before bed clear.**
- **Do not count video calls with grandparents.** Nobody sensible does.

## When it is genuinely a problem

Worth paying attention to if screens are consistently displacing sleep, meals or physical play; if ending a session reliably causes a meltdown out of proportion to everything else; if your child is losing interest in play that involves other people; or if a screen has become the only thing that settles them.

Those are patterns, not single bad afternoons. Every household has bad afternoons.

## The honest summary

Screen time is not neutral, and calling an app educational does not make it free. But a small amount of the right kind, with you nearby, at a time that displaces nothing important, is not something to feel guilty about — and it is a long way from the thing the guilt is usually about.
