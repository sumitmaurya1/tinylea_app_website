You put out something simple. Your child looks at it for two seconds, pushes it away, and says *"I can't."*

They have not tried. That is the part parents find hardest — it looks like laziness, and it almost never is.

## The short answer

"I can't" usually means "I might fail, and you are watching". The useful responses are to make the task smaller rather than easier, to praise what the child did rather than what they are, to wait longer before helping, and to be completely unbothered when they get something wrong.

## What "I can't" actually means

Very occasionally it means the task is genuinely too hard. Far more often it is one of these:

- **"I might get it wrong in front of you."** The stakes feel high because you are there.
- **"I don't know where to start."** The task is clear to you and shapeless to them.
- **"Last time this went badly."** One frustrating session can colour the next five.
- **"I'd rather not try than try and fail."** Not trying protects the idea that they could have done it.

That last one is the most important, and it is the one that hardens if it is not handled.

## Why praising a clever child backfires

It feels natural to say *"you're so clever"* or *"you're such a good reader."* It is a reasonable instinct, and it has a predictable downside.

A child who is told they are clever has something to protect. Attempting something hard risks the label, so the safe move is to avoid hard things — which is exactly the behaviour you were trying to prevent.

A child praised for **what they did** has nothing at stake. Trying something hard and failing is just a thing that happened.

**Instead of:** *"You're so clever."*
**Try:** *"You kept going when that one was tricky."*

**Instead of:** *"Good girl, you got them all right."*
**Try:** *"You checked that one twice before you answered."*

This is less about motivation theory than about what the child concludes is being measured — them, or the work.

<Callout title="The most useful six words">
*"You haven't learned that one yet."* Not "you can't do it" — not yet. It is a small thing to say and it quietly reframes the whole situation: from a fixed fact about the child to a thing that has not happened so far.
</Callout>

## Make it smaller, not easier

The instinct when a child stalls is to substitute something easier. That works for the moment and teaches the wrong lesson — that difficulty is a reason to stop.

Shrinking is better than softening. Same task, less of it:

- Not "let's do something else" but **"just this one, then we'll stop"**
- Not an easier word but **the first sound only**
- Not a different page but **you do one, I'll do one**

The child still meets the hard thing. There is just less of it in front of them at once.

## Wait longer than feels comfortable

Most adults step in after about three seconds. Young children often need ten or more just to organise the attempt.

Count silently to ten before helping. A surprising amount of the time, something happens in seconds four to nine — and the child who got there alone is a different child from the one who was rescued.

If you do step in, do the smallest possible part: start the word and let them finish it.

## Getting it wrong should be boring

How you react to a mistake teaches more than how you react to success.

Aim for mild, matter-of-fact, and quick: *"Close — it's a d. Next one."* No sigh, no long explanation, no *"we did this yesterday!"* Children read tone far better than words, and a disappointed breath lands harder than anything you say.

The same applies to apps and games. An app that plays a sad noise and drops a score is teaching a four-year-old something about being wrong — which is why [it is worth testing what happens on a wrong answer](/blog/how-to-choose-a-learning-app-for-kids) before handing the phone over.

## Let them see you get things wrong

Children assume adults find everything easy, so their own difficulty feels like evidence against them.

Be visibly bad at things in front of them. Get a word wrong on purpose. Say *"hmm, that's tricky, let me think"* out loud. Struggling and carrying on is a far more useful thing to model than always being right.

## When it is frustration and when it is difficulty

Worth telling apart:

**Frustration** looks like: refusing before starting, giving up at the first wobble, fine once they get going, and perfectly capable on a better day.

**Genuine difficulty** looks like: effort that does not pay off, the same thing failing over weeks, tiredness out of proportion to the task, and avoidance that is getting worse rather than better.

The first needs the approach above. The second deserves a word with their teacher — not more practice, which mostly adds frustration to difficulty.

## A short list to keep

- Say *"not yet"* rather than *"you can't"*
- Praise what they did, not what they are
- Make it smaller, never easier
- Count to ten before helping
- Be dull about mistakes
- Let them see you struggle
- Stop while it is still going well

Confidence in learning is built far more by what happens around the mistakes than by what happens around the right answers.
