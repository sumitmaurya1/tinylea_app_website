Festival season is not the time to start a new learning routine. The house is busy, the days are long, relatives are arriving, and nobody has the energy for flashcards.

The good news is that festivals are already packed with the things young children are learning: patterns, counting, colours, shapes, new words and plenty of people to talk to. None of the ideas below add anything to your list. They just make what you are doing anyway count twice.

## 1. Rangoli is a maths lesson

A rangoli is a pattern with symmetry — two of the biggest ideas in early maths, drawn on the floor.

Let your child fill in a section, and talk while they do: *"this side has three dots, so this side needs three too."* For younger children, make a simple repeating pattern they can continue: red, yellow, red, yellow.

Ask them to **say the rule out loud**. Noticing a pattern and putting it into words is [early algebra](/blog/early-maths-after-counting), whatever it looks like on the floor.

## 2. Count the diyas, lights and plates

Counting real things beats counting in the abstract, and festivals supply plenty: diyas to set out, lights to hang, sweets on a plate, chairs for guests.

Keep the numbers small and touch each one. *"How many more do we need?"* is a better question than *"how many are there?"* because it asks the child to work something out.

## 3. Lay the table fairly

Sharing sweets or laying places for guests is division before anyone calls it that.

*"Four people, two sweets each — how many do we need?"* Let them get it wrong and work it out with the actual sweets. The fairness part matters to children, which is exactly why it holds their attention.

## 4. Hunt for colours in the decorations

Festival decorations are brighter than anything else in the house all year.

Pick one colour and find everything that matches it. For toddlers, stay on that one colour for the whole game — [one colour at a time](/blog/how-to-teach-colours-to-toddlers) teaches it faster than switching between several.

## 5. Find the shapes

Diya rims are circles, flags are triangles, boxes of sweets are squares and rectangles, torans are strings of repeated shapes.

Ask *"how many corners?"* rather than *"what shape is that?"* Counting corners is what lets a child recognise a triangle [even when it is tilted or stretched](/blog/how-to-teach-shapes-to-toddlers).

<Callout title="Two minutes, not twenty">
During a festival, every one of these works best as a thirty-second moment in the middle of something else — while you are cooking, while they are helping, while you wait for guests. Nothing here is meant to become an activity you sit down to do.
</Callout>

## 6. Teach the greetings in your home language

Relatives arriving is the single best language opportunity of the year.

Teach your child the greeting, the thank you, and the names for the relatives themselves — nani, dada, kaka, mavshi, whichever your family uses. Practise once before people arrive so they feel ready rather than put on the spot.

Children use a language when it has a real job to do, and greeting a grandparent is as real as it gets. [Two or three languages will not confuse them.](/blog/will-two-languages-confuse-my-child)

## 7. Let them be the helper with two-step jobs

*"Take the plate to Dadi and then bring back the empty one."*

Two instructions in one sentence is precisely the skill reception teachers most want children to arrive with, and festivals are full of genuine errands to practise it on. Resist repeating yourself straight away — give them a moment to hold it.

## 8. Tell the story of the festival

Every festival has a story, and children of three and up can begin to retell it.

Tell it simply, then ask the questions that build comprehension: *"what happened first?"*, *"why did he do that?"*, *"what happened in the end?"* Sequencing a story is the same skill they will need for reading comprehension later, and it works just as well told aloud as read from a book — the approach in [reading aloud](/blog/how-to-read-aloud-to-toddlers) applies either way.

## 9. Put the photos in order

After the day, look at the photos together and put four or five in the order they happened. *"Did we light the diyas before or after dinner?"*

This builds memory, sequencing and time words — yesterday, before, after, last — which are harder for young children than most adults realise.

## 10. Pack a travel game list

Festival season means journeys: to grandparents, to weddings, to other people's houses, with long stretches of waiting in between.

Agree two or three games before you set off so you are not inventing them at the roadside. [The no-toys list](/blog/learning-games-to-play-anywhere-no-toys) has twelve that need nothing but your voice.

## What not to do

- **Don't turn it into lessons.** The moment it feels like school, it stops working and you both lose the festival.
- **Don't force the greetings.** A shy child pushed to perform in front of a room will say less next time, not more.
- **Don't worry about routine.** Late nights and too many sweets for a few days will not undo anything. Normal service resumes afterwards.

The real learning in a festival is not the counting anyway. It is a child seeing where they come from, hearing their family's language spoken all around them, and being part of something — and that happens whether or not anyone counts the diyas.
