Writing their own name is the milestone parents notice most, partly because it is the one that shows up on a drawing and goes on the fridge. It is also the one most likely to be started too early, with a pencil, at a table, and abandoned in tears.

Handwriting is a physical skill before it is a literacy one. Most of the useful work happens before a pencil is involved at all.

## The muscles do the work, not the letters

A child holding a pencil is using small muscles in the hand and fingers that develop on their own timetable. You cannot practise a letter into existence if the hand is not ready to make the movement.

What builds those muscles, none of it involving writing:

- Playdough — rolling, pinching, squashing
- Threading beads, buttoning, using tongs or tweezers
- Tearing paper, using scissors on a thick line
- Pegs, stacking, screwing lids on and off
- Drawing on a vertical surface — an easel, or paper taped to a wall

The vertical surface is the one most people skip and it is the most useful of the lot. Working upright puts the wrist in the position handwriting needs and builds shoulder stability at the same time.

## What a pencil grip should look like at four

Not perfect. A mature tripod grip — pencil resting between thumb and index, supported by the middle finger — is typical somewhere around five or six, and plenty of capable writers never use one at all.

At three and four you will see a whole fist, then four fingers, then gradually fewer. That progression happens on its own if the hand is getting used.

<Callout title="The short-pencil trick">
Snap a crayon in half, or use a golf pencil or a chunky stub. A short pencil physically cannot be held in a fist — there is nowhere for the other fingers to go. It fixes more grips than any amount of correcting does, and it does not involve nagging.
</Callout>

Worth gently redirecting: a thumb wrapped right over the fingers, or a grip so tight the hand tires within a minute. Not worth redirecting: anything that is comfortable, controlled and produces marks the child is happy with.

## Shapes before letters

There is a reasonably consistent order in which children can copy shapes, and letters sit on top of it. Roughly:

1. **Vertical line** — around two
2. **Horizontal line** — a little after
3. **Circle** — around three
4. **Cross** — around four
5. **Square** — four to four and a half
6. **Diagonal line** — four and a half to five
7. **Triangle** — around five

Ages vary widely and this is a sequence, not a schedule. But the practical point holds: a child who cannot yet copy a diagonal will struggle with **K, M, N, V, W, X, Y, Z**, because those letters are diagonals. That is not a letter problem, and drilling the letter will not fix it.

Give them the shape instead. It transfers.

## Then the name, in the right order

Once circles, lines and crosses are comfortable, start the name — and start with the **first letter only**.

- **Capital first letter, lowercase for the rest.** *Aarav*, not *AARAV*. Children taught all-capitals have to unlearn it at school, and unlearning is slower than learning.
- **One letter at a time.** The first letter alone, for as long as it takes, then add the second.
- **Say the letter as they write it.** It ties the shape to the name and the sound, which is work the [alphabet](/blog/how-to-teach-alphabet-to-toddlers) is doing anyway.
- **Big before small.** A finger in sand, a stick in mud, chalk on a wall, a whiteboard marker. Fine control on paper comes last, not first.

## Paper, lines and size

Do not start on lined paper. Lines add a second demand — letter shape *and* placement — and placement is the harder one.

Plain paper, large letters, as much space as they want. Introduce a single baseline when the shapes are reliable, and only then a second line above it. Tracing sheets are useful at this stage as a middle step between copying and writing unaided — there are [free printable ones](https://tinylearnprintables.com) if you want them ready-made.

## Five minutes, and stop early

- **1 minute** — hands: playdough, tongs, something to squeeze
- **2 minutes** — shapes: whichever one is currently just out of reach
- **2 minutes** — the name, or as much of it as they have

Stop while it is still going well. A child who ends a session pleased will start the next one willingly, and willingness is the whole resource you are managing here.

## Reversed letters are normal

**b** and **d** swapping places, an **s** facing backwards, the whole name written right to left — all of this is typical up to around seven, and it is not an early sign of dyslexia on its own.

Correct it lightly, once, and move on. Drawing attention to it repeatedly tends to make a child self-conscious about writing, which costs far more than a backwards **s** does.

## When to ask someone

Worth raising with their teacher or GP: avoiding all drawing and colouring well past four, a hand that tires or hurts within a minute or two, no established hand preference by around five or six, or a grip so tight that pencils and paper are regularly damaged.

Those are worth a conversation with an occupational therapist. Almost everything else is a child whose hands need more months, not more practice.
