Ask a parent how their four-year-old is doing at maths and the answer is almost always a number: *"she can count to fifty."*

Counting is a good start and it is the easiest part to show off. But it is close to a memory trick — a long sequence learned like a song. The maths that school builds on is something else, and most of it can be built at home without a single worksheet.

## The short answer

After counting, the things that matter most are: recognising small quantities without counting, comparing amounts (more, less, the same), putting numbers in order, spotting and making patterns, and adding or taking away with real objects. All five can be practised in ordinary daily life, in a minute at a time.

## Counting is not understanding numbers

The quickest check takes ten seconds. Put out five objects and ask for **three** of them.

Plenty of children who count fluently to fifty will hand you the whole pile, or count "one, two, three" and keep going. That gap is normal, and closing it matters far more than counting higher — which is why [the counting guide](/blog/teaching-kids-to-count-1-to-100) puts so much weight on touching each object once.

## 1. Seeing small amounts without counting

Adults look at a dice and see "four" instantly. Children learn to do this, and it is worth practising directly.

Hold up some fingers for one second, then hide them. How many? Start with one to three, then up to five. Dice, dominoes and playing cards all work.

This matters because a child who *sees* four rather than counting to four has a spare bit of attention left over for the actual maths.

## 2. More, less and the same

Comparing is the foundation of almost everything later, and the language needs teaching as much as the idea.

Use real things: two piles of biscuits, two rows of blocks. Ask *"who has more?"* and then the harder question — *"how do you know?"* Lining things up in pairs to check is a genuine mathematical method, not a shortcut.

**"The same"** is the hardest of the three and the most skipped. Set up equal amounts deliberately, so it comes up often.

<Callout title="Watch out for the spread-out trap">
Put five buttons in a tight row and five spread wide apart. Many four-year-olds will say the spread-out row has more. They are not being silly — they are using size rather than number. Count them together, push them back together, and count again. Doing this a few times is how the idea that number stays the same really lands.
</Callout>

## 3. Putting numbers in order

Numbers live in a fixed order, and children need to feel that, not just recite it.

Stairs are the best tool in the house: count up as you climb, count down as you descend. Lift buttons, house numbers on the walk, pages in a book, the days left until a visit — all of these put numbers in a line that goes somewhere.

Then ask the question that reveals real understanding: *"what comes after six?"* — without starting from one.

## 4. Patterns

Patterns feel like a craft activity and are actually early algebra.

Make a simple repeating pattern with anything to hand: red block, blue block, red block, blue block… and ask what comes next. Then make patterns with sounds and movements — clap, stamp, clap, stamp. Let your child invent one and you guess.

The step that does the most work is asking them to **say the rule**: *"it goes red, blue, red, blue."* Noticing a rule and putting it into words is the skill.

## 5. First adding and taking away

Long before written sums, children can do this with objects.

*"You have two rotis. Here's one more. How many now?"* Then the other direction: *"You had three, you ate one. How many left?"*

The order is: objects they can touch, then fingers, then holding it in their head. Don't rush to the last one — and don't introduce written sums until a child is comfortable doing it out loud.

## Maths talk in ordinary life

Almost all of this fits into things you already do:

- **Setting the table** — how many plates do we need? Is that enough?
- **Sharing** — give everyone two biscuits each, fairly
- **Cooking** — count the spoons, compare the two piles
- **Shopping** — heavier or lighter, more or fewer
- **Tidying up** — sort by size, colour or shape, and say the rule

A minute here and there beats a scheduled session, and it teaches the more useful lesson: that numbers are for something.

## What not to worry about yet

- **Writing numerals neatly.** That's a [handwriting skill](/blog/how-to-teach-a-child-to-write-their-name), and it develops on its own timetable.
- **Reversed numbers.** A backwards 3 is typical well into school.
- **Counting very high.** Counting to twenty with real understanding beats a hundred by rote.
- **Speed.** Quick recall comes with practice, much later. Thinking time is not a problem.

## A five-minute shape for the week

- **Monday** — fingers or dice: how many, without counting
- **Tuesday** — two piles: which has more, and how do you know?
- **Wednesday** — count the stairs up and down
- **Thursday** — make a pattern and say the rule
- **Friday** — one more, one less, with real objects

Keep it spoken, keep it short, and stop while they are still interested.
